## the metaphor is the argument

sheep can be driven from behind, cats cannot, so a cat herder's only move is to arrange things so that where the cats already want to go is also where the work needs to happen.
rainwater's point is that this is a selection effect rather than an attitude problem: the trait that makes someone good at programming, wanting to understand the whole system first-hand and distrusting an instruction they cannot verify, is the same trait that makes them immune to being told.
so the book treats "influence" as the entire job and treats authority as something you spend down rather than something you have.

## the promotion takes your scorecard away

- **the measure changes:** you were paid for code you wrote, and now you are paid for code other people wrote, which arrives later and reads worse than your own would have
- **credibility is the currency:** stay technical enough that the team still respects your read on a design, but off the critical path, because a lead who owns a deliverable is a lead who stops leading the week it slips
- **the calendar fragments:** the interruptions are the job now, not an obstacle to it, which is the collision [[Maker vs Manager Schedule]] describes from the other side
- **you will be bad at this for a while:** you were promoted for a skill that is not the one now being tested, and rainwater is unusually blunt that the first year is a demotion in competence

## assignments are the main lever

the book spends most of its people material on individual differences, because who gets which task is the one decision a lead makes constantly and cannot delegate.
the fast-and-loose developer, the perfectionist who cannot call anything finished, and the one who only comes alive on problems that interest them are all useful, and all ruinous on the wrong task.

- **peer respect outranks the org chart:** a programmer's status comes from the other programmers, so recognition in front of the team is worth more than a title
- **criticise in private, praise in public:** standard advice, but rainwater ties it to the specific fear that programmers carry, being found out as not smart enough
- **the interesting problem is the reward:** access to the work someone wants to do is compensation, and handing it out carelessly is spending money you did not know you had
- **autonomy has to be real:** delegating a task and then reviewing every decision inside it is worse than not delegating, because it costs the time and the trust both

## hiring, and the firing you avoided

- **hire slowly:** an empty seat is cheaper than a bad hire, because the bad hire consumes the team's time as well as their own
- **interview for how they think:** watch someone reason through a problem rather than checking whether they memorised a syntax
- **firing is the bill for a hiring mistake:** rainwater frames it as a failure that already happened, which is what makes it possible to do without cruelty
- **carrying a non-performer is visible:** the team knows before you do, and what they conclude is that the standard is negotiable

## estimates, schedules and meetings

- **estimates are the happy path:** programmers cost the version where nothing goes wrong, so the number is honest and still too small
- **track actuals:** the only cure for optimistic estimates is a record of the last ten, which turns the next negotiation from a debate into an argument from evidence
- **negotiate scope, not the date:** the date usually belongs to someone else, and the feature list is the variable you actually control
- **meetings want the fewest people and an agenda:** the same conclusion the founders in [[top tech leadership productivity and meeting rules]] arrive at by a different road

## managing the people above you

- **translate:** your manager does not want the technical reason, they want what it costs and what it changes, and supplying the reason instead reads as evasion
- **be the shield:** absorb the churn from above and pass down decisions rather than the thrash that produced them
- **bad news travels early:** a slip reported in week two is a plan, and the same slip reported in week six is a failure
- **never let your boss be surprised in public:** rainwater treats this as the single rule that most determines how much room you get

## growing people, and replacing yourself

the last stretch of the book is about the team's careers rather than the project: mentoring, defending a training budget nobody else defends, and deliberately teaching someone else to do your job.
the framing is that a lead who is indispensable has failed, because indispensable means the team cannot function without them and therefore cannot grow past them.

## what has aged, and what has not

it is a 2002 book and it predates the agile vocabulary entirely, so the scheduling and process chapters assume a big up-front plan, a spec that arrives finished, and a programmer working alone in a cubicle.
there is no code review as a social system, no continuous integration, no distributed team, and the tooling advice has aged out completely.
the people half has not aged at all, which is the usual pattern for this genre and the reason it still gets recommended, and it sits alongside [[Maker vs Manager Schedule]] and the rest of the [[books]] list rather than replacing anything in it.
