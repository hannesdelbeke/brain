## conclusion 1: bold and list-chunking formatting have zero effect

false alarm. the closest real contradiction found is [Sclar et al. 2024, "Quantifying Language Models' Sensitivity to Spurious Features in Prompt Design"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11324) (ICLR 2024, the FormatSpread paper) — trivial formatting changes swing few-shot classification accuracy by up to 76 points on LLaMA-2-13B and Falcon, and the sensitivity survives instruction tuning and more few-shot examples. that looks like a direct hit until the setup is checked against candidate 2's claim on three axes: the formatting axis tested is separators, casing, and item delimiters in a few-shot prompt template, not bold/markdown emphasis or list-chunking of document content; the task is few-shot classification, not fact-retrieval-from-a-document; and the models are 2023-era open-source (LLaMA-2, Falcon, GPT-3 family), which the paper itself flags as more format-sensitive than larger/newer ones. all three differences point the same direction candidate 2 already argues — format sensitivity concentrates in older/smaller/weaker models on structural prompt scaffolding, not in bold-decorating document content for a fact-lookup task on a modern small model. worth citing as adjacent evidence that formatting *can* matter, on a different axis, for a different task, on weaker models — not as a contradiction of candidate 2's specific claim.

the Feb 2026 follow-up already cited in the vault, [Structured Context Engineering for File-Native Agentic Systems](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06384v1) (9,649 experiments, 11 models), reinforces rather than threatens candidate 2: frontier models (Claude Opus 4.5, GPT-5.2, Gemini 2.5 Pro) show statistically indistinguishable accuracy across YAML/Markdown/JSON/TOON, while smaller and open-source models still feel format strongly. the one open question this raises for candidate 2's own writeup, not from new literature but from its own data: claude/haiku is the smallest model in Anthropic's current lineup, so it sits ambiguously between "frontier-robust" and "smaller-model-still-sensitive" in this literature's own framing — worth a sentence in the position piece rather than treating haiku's null as automatically frontier-grade evidence.

no academic study was found that isolates bold-density or list-chunking specifically as the LLM-reading variable, for or against — the same gap the vault's own prior-art search already found. nothing changes here.

## conclusion 2: no U-curve at 20 documents

real threat, but not the one it looks like at first. the honest fix is not "the null is wrong," it's "the null is very likely a task-design artifact, and the interpretation attached to it needs to change."

**three lines of 2025-2026 evidence say position bias is alive and architectural, cutting against "modern small models grew out of it":**

- [NoLiMa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167) (Adobe, ICML 2025) tested 13 frontier models claiming 128K+ context. all did well under 1K tokens; ten of thirteen fell below half their short-context baseline by 32K tokens, and even GPT-4o dropped from 99.3% to 69.7%. the paper's whole point is that ordinary needle-in-haystack tests, including the classic lost-in-the-middle shape, look solved only because the needle and the question share literal words — remove that overlap and frontier models degrade badly.
- Chroma's [Context Rot](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot) report (July 2025) ran 18 frontier models (GPT-4.1, Claude 4, Gemini 2.5, Qwen3) and found reliability drops with input length even on simple retrieval, non-uniformly (sudden cliffs, not smooth decay), and worse as the semantic similarity between the query and the target fact goes down.
- [On the Emergence of Position Bias in Transformers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01951) (MIT, ICML 2025) and its 2026 follow-up, [Lost in the Middle at Birth](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10123), argue the U-shaped bias is a geometric property of causal masking plus residual connections — present at random initialization, before any training on any task or document type, and it doesn't go away after training on real QA data. this is a "topological birthright" of every causal transformer, which argues against any model, including a small modern one, having architecturally outgrown it.

if that were the whole picture, it would flatly contradict candidate 2's flat 83.3%. it isn't the whole picture.

**the same 2025-2026 literature also says the effect depends heavily on whether the query has a unique lexical key — and candidate 2's task has exactly that shape:**

- [Facts as First-Class Objects](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17781) (2026) found Claude Sonnet 4.5 gets 100% exact-match accuracy on structured fact lookup from 10 facts up to 7,000 facts, 97.5% of its 200K context window — no lost-in-the-middle dip at all. the explanation given: each fact is a unique (drug, target, assay) tuple, e.g. "erlotinib inhibits EGFR" vs. "erlotinib inhibits HER2" — lexically overlapping but uniquely disambiguated when the query names the full tuple, which the paper says "gives the attention mechanism sufficient signal to disambiguate" regardless of position. a related result on Gemini 2.5 Flash found the same absence of the effect for simple factoid QA.
- NoLiMa's own framing makes the mechanism explicit: standard needle-in-haystack tests (and, by the same logic, the classic Liu et al. setup once the answer phrase closely echoes the query) let a model shortcut via literal string matching instead of genuine position-robust retrieval. degradation only shows up once that lexical overlap is deliberately removed.
- checking Liu et al.'s own methodology against this: NaturalQuestions-Open answers are short factual strings embedded in full Wikipedia prose the model has to actually locate and extract, retrieved by a real dense retriever (Contriever) against genuinely topically-close distractors — a semantic search problem, not an exact-key lookup. a direct replication on Llama-2-7B with the paper's own dataset (["Lost in the Middle" Replicates](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/udDLgexmJffpMjdDu/lost-in-the-middle-replicates), informal but methodologically faithful) reproduced the U-curve, described as an uneven "Nike Swoosh" rather than a textbook U, but a real dip nonetheless.

candidate 2's multi-document test asks "why was serial X493-22 recalled" against 20 documents, where the serial number is a unique, verbatim, exact-match string planted in exactly one document. that is structurally the same shape as the drug-target-assay tuples that erased the effect for Claude Sonnet 4.5, and the opposite shape from Liu et al.'s paraphrased-answer-in-prose retrieval. the near-duplicate reason clusters add semantic difficulty around the answer content, but the retrieval step itself — finding which of 20 documents contains a specific serial number — is a lexical grep-shaped task, not the ambiguous semantic search Liu et al. built the U-curve on.

**the honest interpretation:** the flat 83.3% is not strong evidence that claude/haiku, or small modern models generally, have architecturally outgrown lost-in-the-middle. the architecture literature (MIT ICML 2025, Lost in the Middle at Birth) argues the bias is baked into every causal transformer regardless of size or training. what the flat result more likely shows is that candidate 2's task gives the model an exact-match shortcut — the unique serial number — that lets it locate the answer-bearing document without needing position-robust semantic search, the same shortcut NoLiMa and Facts as First-Class Objects independently identify as the reason ordinary needle-in-haystack-style tests miss the effect that harder, lexically-decoupled tests still find. this doesn't mean formatting robustness is fake or haiku is secretly bad at this — it means candidate 2's fourth experiment tested "can the model find a document by its unique ID," not "can the model retrieve a semantically-buried fact," and those are different questions with different literatures.

**a note on Liu et al.'s document/task-type dependence specifically, since that was the other thing asked:** the effect appears document-type-general (Wikipedia prose replicates it; the theoretical papers say it should hold for any content since it's architectural) but strongly task-type-dependent — single-fact lookup with a unique lexical key across the query and answer washes it out (Facts as First-Class Objects, Gemini factoid QA, arguably NoLiMa's standard-NIAH baseline), while semantic retrieval without that shortcut (Liu et al.'s own setup, NoLiMa's literal-overlap-removed variant) or multi-hop reasoning ([Lost in the Middle, and In-Between](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10079), which found the effect *intensifies* under multi-hop) reliably shows it. that's a real, citable distinction, and it's the one candidate 2's fourth experiment fell on the wrong side of for testing the base effect it wanted to test against.

## what this changes

candidate 2's four formatting nulls stand — nothing found here threatens them, and the new formatting literature (Sclar, the Feb 2026 follow-up) is consistent with the vault's own read that format sensitivity concentrates in older/weaker models on different formatting axes.

the null U-curve needs a different sentence in the writeup than the one currently drafted. [[2026-09-01 designing a true multi-document lost-in-the-middle test for candidate 2]] and the publish plan's candidate 2 section both currently frame the null U-curve as possibly "a modern small model has stopped exhibiting the base lost-in-the-middle degradation at all." the evidence above says that's the less-supported reading — the better-supported one is that the test's unique-serial-number lookup key is a known shortcut that erases the effect independent of model architecture or size, per Facts as First-Class Objects and NoLiMa specifically. both notes have been updated to carry this as the primary explanation, with "haiku may be architecturally more robust" demoted to a secondary, untested possibility.
