## the claim being checked

a search-reranking result found that combining a diffuse "recency" signal with a specific relevance signal has to be additive, not multiplicative, or the diffuse signal swamps the specific one — proven by a rank-flip derivation, measured on real data. the related-work section draws a parallel to bayesian diagnosis: posterior odds are multiplicative in odds form but additive in log-odds space, and a non-diagnostic symptom (likelihood ratio near 1) contributes almost nothing in log-odds space. the section calls this "the same mathematical shape as this paper's finding, not just an analogy," and cites [base-rate neglect and the prosecutor's fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_rate_fallacy) as the real-world failure of getting this wrong.

that citation doesn't hold up, and neither, fully, does the "same mathematical shape" claim.

## the real foundational sources

**i.j. good's irrelevant-evidence theorem** is the actual first-principles reason the likelihood ratio, not some other function, is the correct unit of evidence. good showed that if a piece of evidence T is irrelevant — independent of the hypotheses and of the main evidence E — then the combined weight of evidence (E, T) must equal the weight of E alone, for every possible value of T's own probability. the only class of functions with that property is functions of the ratio of two likelihoods: the likelihood ratio itself. this result is in good's 1950 book [*Probability and the Weighing of Evidence*](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/bayes/1950-good-probabilityandtheweighingofevidence.pdf), restated in his later [survey](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/jack-good/weight-of-evidence.pdf), and carried into forensic-science methodology by aitken and taroni, described in detail in [this nist commentary](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/jres/122/jres.122.027.pdf). this is the citation the paper draft actually wants: it is a proof that a genuinely uninformative signal must contribute nothing, not an analogy or a cognitive-bias anecdote.

**the clinical-epidemiology likelihood-ratio literature** is the standard applied version of the same idea, associated with sackett's evidence-based-medicine program. pretest odds × likelihood ratio = posttest odds, popularized as a bedside tool via [fagan's nomogram](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4744617/) (fagan, "nomogram for bayes's theorem," nejm 1975) and taught through sackett's jama "users' guides to the medical literature" series, summarized in [this bmj-style overview](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC478236/). a likelihood ratio near 1 means a positive result barely moves pretest probability at all — the direct clinical analogue of "a non-diagnostic symptom contributes almost nothing."

**kahneman & tversky's 1973 "on the psychology of prediction"** (*psychological review* 80(4), 237–251, [apa record](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2Fh0034747)) is the real base-rate-neglect source, not the wikipedia page about it. worth being precise about what it actually shows: subjects predicting which field a described graduate student ("tom w.") belonged to tracked *representativeness* (how well the description matched a stereotype) almost perfectly, and barely moved at all when the base rate of each field was changed. that is neglect of the *prior odds term* — people substitute a similarity judgment for bayesian combination entirely, rather than running the update in the wrong space. it is adjacent to the paper's point, not the same claim.

## where the math actually diverges

the paper draft's stronger claim is that the rank-flip proof and bayesian log-odds combination are "the same mathematical shape." working through both sides:

bayes' rule in odds form is `posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio`. taking logs gives `log(posterior odds) = log(prior odds) + log(likelihood ratio)`. this is not a separate, safer way of combining evidence — it is the identical computation, relabeled. logarithm is a strictly monotonic function, so for any two hypotheses (or any two candidates being compared by their posterior odds), the ranking by odds and the ranking by log-odds are *always* the same ranking. there is no way to "do it wrong" in odds space and get a different ranking than doing it "right" in log-odds space. multiplying by a likelihood ratio near 1 is a small effect regardless of which space you view it in — the smallness is a property of the number 1, not of choosing addition over multiplication.

the search-reranking rank-flip proof is a genuinely different kind of claim. it compares two *non-equivalent* scoring functions — `final = v × p` (multiplicative) against `final = v + λp` (additive) — and shows they can rank the same pair of candidates in opposite orders. that is only possible because `v × p` is not a monotonic function of `v + λp` (or vice versa): they are two different functions of the same inputs, not two encodings of the same number. the proof's entire force comes from that non-equivalence — a rival candidate with high proximity can outscore the true target multiplicatively while losing additively, for a specific, derived reason. bayesian odds-space and log-odds-space cannot diverge that way by construction, because log is monotonic and multiplication-in-odds-space is definitionally the same update as addition-in-log-space.

put plainly: the reranking paper has two competing, non-equivalent design choices and proves one dominates the other. bayes' theorem has one computation and two equivalent ways to write it down. "the same mathematical shape" overstates this — it is a real, useful, first-principles-flavored analogy (both cases feature a term that should stay near-neutral, and a representation that makes near-neutral visibly near-neutral), but not a proof-level correspondence, and the divergence above is exactly where it breaks, not just a hand-wave.

there is a second, smaller mismatch worth naming: a "non-diagnostic" symptom in the bayesian case is assumed to have a likelihood ratio near 1 *for every hypothesis* — that is what makes it diffuse. the reranking paper's diffuse signal (temporal proximity) is not uniformly near-neutral across candidates; it ranges from near 0 (temporally distant) to near 1 (temporally close), and the failure mode is precisely a rival candidate where it is *not* neutral. a genuinely uniform, always-near-1 diffuse term, bayesian-style, would never be able to flip a rank under either combination rule — the reranking failure needs the diffuse signal to vary a lot across candidates, which is a different setup than "a common symptom with ratio near 1 across patients."

## no third field found making this exact comparison

searched for the same structural point — combining a diffuse, low-information signal with a specific one, additive-in-the-right-space avoids domination, multiplicative-in-the-wrong-space causes it — outside search and bayesian diagnosis: sensor fusion, ensemble methods, recommender systems, forecasting, information-retrieval score fusion (comb{sum,mnz}, [aslam & montague's bayesian metasearch](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-007-9021-x)), forensic evidence combination. nothing surfaced making this exact point in those terms. two related but weaker matches:

- **credit scoring** (weight of evidence / information value scorecards) is the same underlying math applied elsewhere, not an independently-derived parallel: woe is literally a log-likelihood-ratio per predictor bin, and the whole point of the scorecard is additivity in log-odds space so a weak predictor's contribution (information value under 0.02, per the [standard threshold](https://www.listendata.com/2015/03/weight-of-evidence-woe-and-information.html)) stays visibly small. it's a second application of good's theorem, not a second, independent proof of the same shape.
- **robust control theory** (Åström & Murray, already cited in the draft) looked like the one genuine match — a different mathematical framework, feedback-loop stability margins, reaching a similar-sounding conclusion. a parallel, separately-run check of that citation ([[2026-09-01 the robust control theory parallel, worked through properly]]) read the cited chapter in full and found it doesn't hold either, for a different reason than the bayesian case: "gain peaking" and "conditional instability" aren't terms the chapter uses, and mainstream control practice treats multiplicative uncertainty as the *preferred*, more realistic model for unmodeled dynamics, not the risky one — the opposite emphasis from what the draft claimed. that note's own conclusion is worth stating precisely here, since it changes what's left of the cross-domain case: the one fact that survives in both the bayesian and control-theory tellings is the same underlying arithmetic, not two independent proofs — a bounded additive term can only change a *difference* by that bound, while a bounded multiplicative term changes a *ratio* by that bound but can change the difference by an amount that scales with the quantities themselves. bayes reaches that fact via an identity (log is monotonic, so odds-space and log-odds-space can never actually diverge); control theory reaches it via two genuinely different perturbation models that can diverge, but the field's own practice leans multiplicative, not additive; the reranking paper's rank-flip proof is the only one of the three where the two combination rules are non-equivalent functions *and* the paper's own preferred one (additive) is the one that wins.

## the counter-example: additive is not universally safe either

the task's own framing is correct and worth stating directly: bayesian updating is fundamentally multiplicative in odds/probability space. log-odds additivity is a change of coordinates, not a separately safer algorithm — you cannot choose to do bayesian updating "the multiplicative way" as a mistake, because there is no other way to do it; the addition is just the multiplication written differently.

but there is a real failure mode that shows additive log-odds combination is not a free safety guarantee: naive bayes assumes every piece of evidence is conditionally independent given the hypothesis, which licenses summing individual log-likelihood-ratios. when that assumption breaks — correlated or redundant evidence, treated as if independent — the additive sum double-counts the shared signal and produces systematically overconfident, miscalibrated posteriors, a well-documented effect in the machine-learning literature (surveyed in [zhang's flairs-04 paper on naive bayes optimality](https://www.cs.unb.ca/~hzhang/publications/FLAIRS04ZhangH.pdf), building on domingos & pazzani's 1997 result that classification accuracy can survive the independence violation even though the probability estimates do not). forensic evidence combination hits the identical wall: aitken and taroni's likelihood-ratio framework for combining multiple evidence types explicitly requires the evidence to be independent, and when it isn't, the field's own answer is to move to bayesian networks rather than keep summing log-likelihood-ratios naively.

that failure has the same shape as the reranking paper's own rival-density mechanism, read the other way around: many correlated, individually-diffuse-looking pieces of evidence, each contributing "almost nothing," can still swamp the correct posterior once summed, if they are not actually independent. additive-in-log-space is safe against one diffuse term with a fixed, bounded weight — exactly the reranking paper's own additive recency term — but not automatically safe against many diffuse terms that turn out to be correlated. the paper's practical recommendation (a single small additive λ, bounded) survives this caveat; the broader implication that "additive combination is the structurally safe form" does not, without that qualifier.

## verdict

the cross-domain bayesian parallel is a useful pedagogical analogy, not the proof-level equivalence the draft currently claims, and the citation backing it (base-rate neglect, prosecutor's fallacy) describes a different error — neglecting the prior, or inverting a conditional — not the additive-vs-multiplicative combination-space question the paper is actually making a claim about. the real supporting citations are i.j. good's irrelevant-evidence theorem, the clinical likelihood-ratio literature, and, for the base-rate-neglect name itself, kahneman & tversky 1973 directly rather than its wikipedia summary. the control-theory parallel, checked separately, doesn't hold up either, and for a sharper reason: mainstream control practice prefers multiplicative uncertainty models, the opposite emphasis from the draft's claim. what both cross-domain sections are actually pointing at, once corrected, is one piece of elementary arithmetic — bounded additive terms cap a difference, bounded multiplicative terms cap a ratio but not a difference — dressed in two different fields' vocabulary. that's a real, citable fact, just a much smaller claim than "two independent proofs from unrelated fields," and the paper should say so plainly rather than lean on either field for first-principles weight it can't fully supply.
