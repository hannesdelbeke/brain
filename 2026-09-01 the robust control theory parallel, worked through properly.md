## the actual definitions

a feedback loop has a nominal process $P$ and a real, imperfectly-known process. [Åström and Murray's *Feedback Systems*, chapter 12](https://www.cds.caltech.edu/~murray/books/AM08/pdf/am08-robperf_19Jul11.pdf) gives three ways to represent the gap, not two: additive ($P + \Delta$), multiplicative ($P(1+\delta)$, with $\delta = \Delta/P$), and feedback ($P/(1+\Delta_{fb}\cdot P)$). the small-gain theorem gives each one its own, different stability condition, not a shared "bounded good, unbounded bad" split:

- additive: $\|CS\Delta\|_\infty < 1$
- multiplicative: $\|T\delta\|_\infty < 1$
- feedback: $\|PS\Delta_{fb}\|_\infty < 1$

$S$ and $T$ here are the sensitivity and complementary sensitivity functions of the loop, and $C$ is the controller. none of the three conditions says "this type of uncertainty is inherently unbounded." each says a specific closed-loop transfer function, weighted by the uncertainty, has to stay under 1 in norm. the type of uncertainty determines which transfer function does the weighting, not whether the system is safe in general.

one number in the chapter does support half of the parallel: for additive uncertainty, the tolerable size is $|\Delta| < |P|/|T|$ (equation 12.6). that bound scales with $|P|$, the plant's own gain. so additive uncertainty is not "self-limiting regardless of the underlying signal's size," as a looser reading might imply — the tolerance itself is proportional to how big the nominal signal already is. what's actually invariant is the *relative* tolerance for multiplicative uncertainty, $|\delta| < 1/|T|$, which doesn't reference $|P|$ at all. that's the real, precise version of "one type tracks the signal's scale and the other partly does" — worth keeping, but it is not the same claim as "additive shifts margins by a fixed amount, multiplicative destabilizes."

## two claims that don't survive a close read

**"gain peaking."** this exact phrase does not appear anywhere in chapter 12 of *Feedback Systems* — not in the text, the examples, the further-reading section, or the exercises. the actual peaking phenomenon is a different, later piece of control theory: [Sussmann and Kokotović's 1991 paper](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/76384) on high-gain state feedback and high-gain observers in nonlinear systems, where driving observer gain very high causes a transient that can interact badly with growth in the plant's nonlinearity. it has nothing to do with choosing additive versus multiplicative uncertainty models for a linear loop. citing chapter 12 for "gain peaking" attributes a real term from a different subfield to a source that never uses it.

**"conditional instability."** also absent from the chapter as a named concept. the chapter does show designs (the vehicle-steering pole-placement example) where a well-damped nominal design turns out to have almost no robustness margin, driven by pole/zero placement relative to process dynamics — a real and relevant phenomenon, but described there through sensitivity peaks ($M_s$, $M_t$), not the term "conditional instability."

## multiplicative uncertainty is the standard choice, not the risky one

the strongest correction: mainstream robust-control practice treats multiplicative (relative) uncertainty as the *preferred* model for unmodeled dynamics, not a mode to avoid. [Doyle, Francis, and Tannenbaum's *Feedback Control Theory*](https://www.control.utoronto.ca/people/profs/francis/dft.pdf) — the standard reference chapter 12 itself builds on — picks multiplicative perturbation for its detailed study specifically because it's the typical case: real unmodeled high-frequency dynamics produce relative error that grows with frequency, which a multiplicative weight captures naturally and a fixed additive offset does not. course material from MIT and other robust-control curricula makes the same point directly: unstructured uncertainty from neglected dynamics is normally modeled multiplicatively, and even when engineers use an additive weight for convenience, they still shape it to grow at high frequency to mimic multiplicative behavior. additive uncertainty is reserved for a narrower case — uncertain terms confined to the numerator of a specific parametric family — not offered as the generally safer default.

the small-gain theorem itself long predates this chapter: [George Zames's 1966 two-part paper](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1098316) proved the general input-output stability result the chapter's argument specializes. nothing in that foundational line of work ranks one uncertainty type as safer in general; it treats both as equally legitimate models of physical reality, chosen by which one matches how the real unmodeled dynamics actually behave.

## is a ranking comparison actually a feedback loop

no, and this is the part worth being direct about. a feedback loop's stability margin is a statement about a dynamical system: a signal recirculates through the loop, and the small-gain theorem bounds how much a perturbation to one block can shrink the distance between the loop's frequency response and the point that causes instability, evaluated across all frequencies. there is no recirculation, no frequency response, and no stability question in a static rank-flip argument over two candidate scores. computing whether $v_j + p_j > v_i + p_i$ swaps against $v_j \times p_j > v_i \times p_i$ for fixed numbers is an algebra problem about one comparison, not a dynamical system that can oscillate, drift, or blow up over iterations.

what the two domains do share is a fact one level more general than either of them: adding a bounded term to two quantities can only change their *difference* by that bound, while multiplying by a bounded factor changes their *ratio* by that bound but can change their *difference* by an amount that grows with the quantities themselves. that fact is true of any pair of comparable numbers — it is why it also shows up in Bayesian odds versus log-odds, and it is why it shows up in a rank-flip proof over vector-similarity scores. control theory's small-gain theorem is one more place this same elementary fact gets dressed in loop-transfer-function notation; it is not independent confirmation that comes from a different kind of mathematical object, because underneath the notation it is the identical arithmetic fact, not a separate theorem about stability that happens to agree.

## where multiplicative combination actually wins

the honest counter-example is the same fact that makes multiplicative uncertainty the control-theory default: when the thing being modeled genuinely scales with the base signal — relative measurement error, percentage-based drift, unmodeled dynamics whose magnitude tracks the plant's own gain — a multiplicative model is more accurate, not just more dangerous. an additive correction sized for a small base signal is too small once the base signal is large, and an additive correction sized for a large base signal massively overcorrects a small one. this is exactly why calibrated measurement systems, gain-scheduled controllers, and log-odds-style probability combination all reach for multiplicative or ratio-based combination once they know the uncertainty scales with the signal. "additive is safer" only holds when the diffuse signal being added does *not* legitimately scale with whatever it's being combined with — which has to be argued for the specific case at hand, not assumed as a general rule borrowed from control theory.

## verdict

the parallel is a loose borrowing of vocabulary, not a tight mathematical analogy. what holds: the elementary arithmetic fact that a bounded additive term limits absolute change while a bounded multiplicative term limits relative (not absolute) change, which is real, citable, and does appear inside the small-gain theorem's bookkeeping. what doesn't hold: the specific citations to "gain peaking" and "conditional instability," which are not in the cited chapter; the implication that multiplicative combination is control theory's cautionary case, when mainstream practice treats it as the preferred model for realistic unmodeled dynamics; and the framing of "additive is self-limiting regardless of signal size," when the chapter's own bound shows the additive tolerance scales with the plant's gain too. a paper leaning on this parallel for first-principles support should cite the elementary arithmetic fact directly rather than dress it in a control-theory citation that, read closely, argues the opposite emphasis about which combination mode practitioners actually prefer.
